01

The big picture

What point-of-sale financing is, who the four players are, and why a multi-lender waterfall is the whole point.

Point-of-sale (POS) financing is credit offered at the moment of purchase, inside the merchant's own experience — online checkout, an in-store terminal, or a technician's tablet at a customer's kitchen table. Embedded financing is the broader idea: regulated lending products delivered inside a non-bank experience instead of at a bank.

The four players

  • Consumer — the applicant during decisioning; the borrower / account holder once credit exists.
  • Merchant — the business selling the goods or services: a big-box home-improvement retailer like The Home Depot, or a home-services franchise network like Authority Brands, whose technicians offer financing on a $6,000 HVAC replacement in the customer's home.
  • Lender — the regulated entity that extends credit, owns the credit decision, and is the legal creditor. In our ecosystem that spans bank issuers (Wells Fargo, Citi), home-improvement installment lenders (GreenSky, Goodleap, Foundation Finance), and second-look specialists (Fortiva).
  • Platform — the orchestration layer connecting one merchant integration to many lenders. It routes applications, normalizes offers, runs the checkout and postsale transaction lifecycle, and reconciles the money. The platform is generally not the lender.

The lender waterfall

A single application cascades through lenders ordered roughly by credit tier: prime (bank-issued PLCC, DLOC, prime installment) → near-prime installment lenders → second-look / subprime specialists → lease-to-own as the final rung. "First look" is the lender who sees the application first; a "second look" lender only receives applications an upstream lender declined. Every extra rung converts sales that would otherwise walk away — a well-built waterfall approves the large majority of applicants (ChargeAfter cites up to ~85%), and that conversion lift is the commercial core of a multi-lender platform versus a single-lender program.

Step zero — the returning-customer lookup. Before submitting a new application, the platform checks the consumer's PII for an existing account with available credit. If one is found (often confirmed via a one-time passcode, §03), the consumer can pay with it immediately — no new application, no new credit pull. Only when no usable account is found does a fresh application enter the waterfall.

Adjacent single-lender BNPL names you'll hear in the industry: Affirm, Klarna, Afterpay, Sezzle, Splitit. They compete for the same checkout button — the multi-lender model's answer is breadth of the waterfall rather than one balance sheet.

Why merchants pay for this

Financing lifts approval rates, checkout conversion, and average order value (AOV). Merchants fund promotional plans through a merchant fee / merchant discount rate (MDR) or a subsidy ("buy-down") — for example, paying the lender a few percent of the ticket so the consumer can get "0% for 12 months." That fee shows up again in §07 when we reconcile it.

02

Products

Two axes classify almost everything: open-end (revolving) vs closed-end (installment), and secured vs unsecured.

Installment loan

closed-end · unsecured

A fixed amount, fixed term, and (usually) fixed monthly payments; one loan per purchase or project. Home-improvement "project loans" — the GreenSky / Goodleap / Foundation Finance space — are typically unsecured installment loans sold through contractors and dealers.

Revolving line of credit (RLOC)

open-end

A credit limit the consumer can draw against, repay, and re-draw — internally often called an RLOC. Balances revolve; minimum payments apply; APR accrues on carried balances; available credit replenishes as payments post. PLCCs and DLOCs are both revolving products.

Lender RLOCs are classified by how the consumer is billed for a purchase: Flexible Payments (pay at your own pace, subject to minimum monthly payments) vs Equal Payments (the purchase is split into equal monthly payments). Neither carries prepayment penalties or minimum purchase requirements. Fees and late-payment terms live in the lender's Summary of Terms / cardholder agreement disclosures, shown before the consumer accepts.

PLCC — Private Label Credit Card

open-end · card

A store-branded revolving card usable only at that merchant ("closed-loop"), issued and owned by a partner bank (Citi Retail Services, Synchrony, Wells Fargo, TD, and similar). The merchant gets branding, loyalty, and promo financing; the bank owns the credit relationship, the receivables, and the compliance burden. Contrast with a co-branded card, which carries a network mark (Visa/Mastercard) and works anywhere.

DLOC — Digital Line of Credit

open-end · digital

Think "PLCC without the plastic": a revolving account issued digitally at checkout — instant account number, no physical card, used card-not-present inside the merchant or platform experience. Banks use DLOCs to compete in embedded financing without card fulfillment. From the platform's perspective a DLOC behaves like any revolving account you transact against through the postsale lifecycle (§06).

BNPL / pay-in-4

short-term split pay

Four payments over roughly six weeks, usually interest-free to the consumer and funded by merchant fees. A different animal from long-term installment financing; historically lightly regulated, now converging toward credit-style (Reg Z-like) treatment.

Lease-to-own (LTO)

not credit

The provider buys the goods and leases them to the consumer with an option to own. There is no APR — instead a disclosed "cost of leasing," which can be high. LTO serves no-credit and deep-subprime consumers, is regulated as a lease rather than a loan, and sits on the final rung of the waterfall. Terms are typically fixed at 3, 6, or 12 months; after all lease payments, the consumer automatically owns the items. At any point they have three options: keep leasing, buy out the items (part of every lease payment counts toward the buyout), or return them with no further obligation beyond past-due amounts.

Early purchase option: during roughly the first 90 days, the consumer can buy the items out for a small percentage above cash price (~5–10%, varies by lender; California residents pay just the cash price). Initial payment: collected at checkout on a credit/debit card — most lenders treat the flat amount as a processing fee that does not count toward the buyout. Periodic payments are scheduled from the consumer's paycheck frequency and dates, billed to the card on file.

Non-leasable items: LTO only fits leaseable goods. Typical exclusions: non-durable/disposable goods, items under ~$20, product warranties and protection plans, assembly services, non-tangible goods, and recycling/government fees. A cart containing them may be declined by some lenders — others approve but charge the non-leasable portion upfront (added to the initial payment). Side note: because the lessor buys the goods from the merchant, LTO orders are B2B sales and the lender is exempt from sales tax.

HELOC — Home Equity Line of Credit

open-end · secured

A revolving line secured by home equity: typically a draw period (~10 years, low minimums, often variable rate) followed by a repayment period (~10–20 years). Because it's secured, rates are far lower and limits far higher than unsecured POS products — but origination takes weeks (appraisal, title, closing), so it's not an instant-checkout product. It matters to us because big-ticket home improvement is exactly where HELOCs and unsecured project loans compete, and some lender ecosystems cross-offer both. Cousins: the HELoan (closed-end home equity loan) and cash-out refinance.

Two HELOC mechanics worth knowing: the variable rate is built as index + margin (a public benchmark like the Prime Rate plus a fixed markup — same construction some POS revolving products use), and many lenders offer a fixed-rate lock option — converting a chunk of the variable balance into its own fixed-rate sub-loan with its own payment. That lock is the same mental model as a promo plan per purchase on a PLCC: one revolving account, several fixed "plans" living inside it.

Down payment, LTV & PMI

secured lending mechanics

Vocabulary you'll meet whenever a conversation crosses into secured lending. A down payment is the upfront percentage of the purchase price the borrower pays at closing (typically 5–20% on mortgages) — their stake in the asset; the bigger it is, the smaller the loan and usually the better the rate. LTV (loan-to-value) = loan amount ÷ the lower of appraised value or purchase price; when LTV exceeds 80%, lenders typically require PMI (private mortgage insurance) — an extra monthly cost protecting the lender. CLTV (combined LTV) adds a proposed second lien on top of the existing mortgage — HELOC lenders typically cap it around 85% — and the appraisal supplies the value in the denominator.

Why it's in this guide: these ratios gate how much a consumer can borrow against a home, so they shape every HELOC/HELoan offer our big-ticket flows compete with. They also have POS cousins — LTO's initial payment, and the down payments some installment programs collect at checkout — same idea: skin in the game reduces the lender's exposure.

Refinancing & end of draw

loan lifecycle

Refinancing = replacing an existing loan with a new one. Two flavors: rate-and-term (same money, better terms — lower rate or longer term to cut the monthly payment) and cash-out (a bigger new first mortgage; the equity difference is paid out as a lump sum — the main competitor to a HELOC for tapping home equity). The consumer math is a break-even: closing costs ÷ monthly savings = months until the refi pays for itself.

End of draw is the HELOC's built-in cliff: when the draw period ends, minimum payments jump from interest-only to principal + interest (or, on balloon HELOCs, the whole balance comes due). The standard options are refinance into a new HELOC, start the repayment period, pay off early, or a payment-modification program.

Why it's in this guide: refinancing is where lending's competitive pressure lives — a booked loan is never final — and end-of-draw is the classic example of a servicing-time payment shock, the kind of event support and product teams design around.

For engineers

Product type drives system behavior: revolving products have open-to-buy and can host many purchases; installment products are one-loan-per-purchase; LTO isn't credit and skips bureau furnishing. Model the two axes (open/closed end, secured/unsecured) explicitly — most downstream branching follows from them.

03

Application & credit decisioning

Application vs account, co-applicants, prequalification, soft vs hard pulls, frozen files, and what a decision legally requires.

Application vs Account

the core distinction

An application is a request for credit plus its decisioning journey: applicant data, consents, bureau results, decisions, offers. An account is the funded credit relationship that exists only after the applicant accepts an offer and the lender books (originates) it — with an account number, a limit or loan amount, terms, a balance, statements, and a servicing lifecycle.

One consumer can hold many applications over time; each accepted application books one account. Transactions (auth/settle/refund) happen against accounts, never applications. Approved applications carry an offer validity window (often ~30 days) and an approved amount that may differ from the requested amount.

Applicant & co-applicant

joint credit

A co-applicant applies jointly: both incomes and credit profiles count (improving debt-to-income, approval odds, and credit limit) and both are fully liable for the debt, which typically reports on both credit files. Distinguish from a cosigner (liable, not necessarily a user) and an authorized user (can spend, not liable).

Under ECOA / Reg B, joint intent must be affirmatively captured — that's why the "we intend to apply jointly" checkbox exists — and a lender can't require a spouse's signature just because an applicant is married. Adverse-action notices go to the co-applicant too.

For engineers

Co-applicant flows double the surface area: two PII sets, two KYC verifications, two credit-pull consents, two e-signatures, two adverse-action deliveries. Model the applicant as a repeated entity from day one — retrofitting "applicant #2" onto a single-applicant schema is a classic painful migration.

Prequalification vs preapproval vs application

funnel stages

Prequalification: a soft-pull, non-binding indication — "you're likely to qualify for up to $X." No score impact, no commitment. On a multi-lender platform, prequal can sweep the whole waterfall softly and present comparable offers before any hard inquiry. Preapproval is stronger — often a firm offer of credit subject to verification. Marketing blurs the two; internally, be precise. The full application produces final terms, normally with a hard pull at acceptance/booking.

Soft pull vs hard pull

credit inquiries

A soft pull doesn't affect the score and isn't visible to other lenders — used for prequalification and account reviews. A hard pull is recorded on the file, visible to other lenders, and dings the score slightly; it requires clear consumer initiation and happens when a real credit decision is made — on our flows, typically once, at acceptance with the chosen lender. That's the consumer-friendly property of the model: one hard pull despite a five-lender waterfall. Both are governed by FCRA (permissible purpose, disclosure, the consumer's right to see inquiries).

Loan stacking

risk

Taking out multiple loans from different lenders in a short window, exploiting the lag before new inquiries and tradelines show up at the bureaus — so each lender underwrites blind to the others. It ranges from a consumer over-borrowing to organized first-party fraud (many maxed applications in days, no intent to repay). Lenders defend with velocity checks (how many applications/inquiries recently?), inquiry monitoring, and cross-lender data consortiums.

Why it's in this guide: our waterfall looks superficially similar — one consumer, many lenders — but it's the opposite: soft-pull prequal, one accepted offer, one hard pull, and the platform sees every lender's decision in one place. That cross-lender visibility is actually an anti-stacking advantage isolated lenders don't have. Keep the distinction sharp: waterfall = comparison shopping; stacking = parallel borrowing the lenders can't see.

Returning consumer & OTP

identity

A consumer who already holds an account doesn't reapply — the platform locates the account from their PII and verifies it's really them with a one-time passcode (OTP): a short code sent by text, email, or phone call that they enter in the UI. Once verified, they can pay with the existing account's available credit, skipping the application (and any credit pull) entirely. If the lookup misses (details entered differently) or available credit is insufficient, a new application enters the waterfall.

Credit freeze

bureau state

A security freeze is a consumer-initiated block at a bureau that stops new creditors from reading the file — so a frozen file usually means the lender cannot decision the application and returns a frozen-file outcome. Freezes are free by federal law and the consumer must lift ("thaw") them per bureau — often online in minutes. Related but different: a fraud alert (file readable, but extra identity verification required) and a credit lock (a bureau's proprietary on/off product; similar effect, contractual rather than statutory).

Good platform UX detects the frozen-file reason, tells the user which bureau to thaw, and lets the application resume — this recovers real conversions.

Bureaus, scores & decision outcomes

decisioning

Three national bureaus — Experian, Equifax, TransUnion — feed scores like FICO and VantageScore. A thin file / no-hit means not enough history to score — one reason second-look and alternative-data lenders exist. Decisions come back approved (sometimes as a counter-offer with a different amount or term), declined, or pended for manual review (income docs, ID verification).

An adverse action notice (FCRA + ECOA) must follow a decline or counter-offer — principal reasons, the bureau used, score disclosure — within 30 days. This is why decline reasons are structured data, never free text, and why "silently decline" is not an option.

Also in this stage

KYC / identity verification (CIP under the BSA / USA PATRIOT Act — verify name, DOB, address, SSN/ITIN), OFAC sanctions screening, fraud and velocity checks, and MLA (Military Lending Act) covered-borrower status checks all run alongside the credit decision. All of it runs on PII (personally identifiable information: full name, SSN, phone, email, billing/shipping address) — the most sensitive data class the platform handles, moved only over approved secure channels.

04

Offers & rate plans

What lenders return, how promotional financing is priced, and the two tools consumers see before ever applying.

Offer

decisioning output

What a lender returns for an application: product type + approved amount + one or more rate plans. The platform normalizes offers across lenders so a consumer can compare "$104/mo for 36 months at 9.99% APR" against "0% for 12 months, equal payments" side by side. Offers expire (validity window) and can be conditional pending verification.

Rate plans (promotional / financing plans)

pricing

A rate plan is a priced set of terms, usually identified by a lender plan code, that a merchant enables for given products, categories, or ticket sizes ("6/12/18-month options above $299"). The three families you must be able to tell apart:

  • Standard APR — the ordinary purchase APR; revolving minimums or a fixed installment schedule.
  • 0% APR equal payments ("equal pay," "X months promotional financing") — truly no interest; the balance divides into equal monthly payments. The merchant typically pays the lender a subsidy / buy-down for it. No retroactive interest, ever.
  • Deferred interest ("no interest if paid in full in X months," "same-as-cash") — interest accrues from day one but is waived if the full promo balance is paid before the promo ends. Miss it by a dollar or a day and the entire accrued interest lands retroactively.

Internally you'll also meet promo-type shorthand pulled from lender configuration — codes like FP (flexible payments), EPNI (equal payments, no interest), and WPDI (deferred interest with payments) — these drive which widget and promotional modal a product page shows.

The classic trap

Deferred interest ≠ 0% equal pay. Deferred interest is the most misunderstood consumer product in this industry: minimum payments alone usually do not pay the balance off within the promo window. Reg Z / CARD Act impose specific disclosure and payment-allocation rules on it, and support teams field the fallout every promo-expiry season. Keep the two plan families straight in copy, in code, and on calls.

Anatomy of a real rate plan

worked example

This is what a plan actually looks like as data — a lightly trimmed real plan object from a lender program (a 120-month installment loan with a 6-month 0% promo):

{
  "planId": "1065",
  "category": "Mixed Interest Rate, 0% Promo Period",
  "minInterestRate": "5.99",  "maxInterestRate": "5.99",
  "paymentfactor": "1.11",    "promoPaymentFactor": "0.00",
  "term": 126,                "promotionMonths": "6",
  "purchaseMonths": "6",      "merchantFee": "24.25",
  "creditProduct": "Installment",
  "ficoMinimum": 0,           "ficoMaximum": 1000,
  "minimumLoanAmount": "1000.00", "maximumLoanAmount": "250000.00",
  "prequalMultiOfferPlan": false, "ficoBased": false, "solar": false
}

How to read it:

  • Payment factor — the monthly payment as a percentage of principal: factor 1.11 means $11.10/month per $1,000 financed. It's precomputed amortization (P·r/(1−(1+r)^−n)), so estimators and reps never do the math live. promoPaymentFactor: 0.00 = no payments due during the promo.
  • APR ≠ interest rate — this plan's disclosure says 5.40% APR even though the note rate is 5.99%: six months at 0% with no payments dilute the total cost of credit, and the APR is the rate that makes the whole cash flow balance. A promo can make the disclosed APR lower than the rate the consumer eventually pays — the single best concrete example of the APR/interest-rate distinction.
  • term: 126 = 6 promo months + 120 amortized payments — the term field counts the whole life, not just the paying part.
  • merchantFee is the buy-down, visible — on the same 120-month term, a 5.99% consumer rate costs the merchant 24.25% of the ticket while a 9.99% rate costs 14.25%; stretching the 0% promo from 6 to 12 months raises the fee again (24.25 → 27.00). Cheap money for the consumer is bought with merchant subsidy — you can watch the trade-off directly in the plan table.
  • purchaseMonths — the purchase window: the loan is approved once, but new purchases can be charged against it for N months. That makes it a multi-draw installment loan — vital for home improvement, where materials and labor bill over weeks (§10).
  • Three text fields, three audiences: description (internal/marketing label), productDescription (plain-language mechanics), productDisclosure (the Reg Z legal text — naming the servicer, the NMLS number, and the program-lender model: "the platform is not the lender; credit decisions are made by program lenders").
  • Config flags: ficoMinimum/Maximum and ficoBased (rate-by-score plans), prequalMultiOfferPlan (eligible for multi-offer prequal), min/max loan amounts, solar (solar-vertical plans have their own shapes), activationStatus (plans are versioned config — they get turned on and off).

Payment estimator

pre-purchase tool

The calculator that turns (ticket amount, APR, term) into an estimated monthly payment: standard amortization — P·r / (1 − (1+r)^−n) — for installment plans, or simple division for 0% equal-pay. It powers "as low as $52/mo" messaging. The assumptions (representative plan, best-qualified APR) matter, which is why estimator copy gets compliance review.

Promotional widget

pre-purchase tool

An embeddable component on product, cart, and checkout pages showing financing options before any application exists — "As low as $58/mo" plus a prequalify call-to-action. It runs offer-calculation rules against the ticket and the merchant's enabled plans. Consumers who see a monthly payment early are far more likely to finance, which makes the widget one of the highest-leverage conversion surfaces on the platform.

Clicking the widget opens the promotional modal — the screen with full details of the promotion(s) the lender offers for that merchant, and usually the entry into the apply flow. Widgets come in configurable modes: price-focused (monthly price highlighted — the most common), brand-focused (the financing brand highlighted next to the price), multi-promotion (several offers shown), and no-promo (links out to a financing page instead of opening the modal). The widget's type follows the promo plan behind it (FP / EPNI / WPDI), which comes from lender configuration.

Financing brands & white label

program branding

The financing option appears at checkout as a branded payment method, and the brand depends on the merchant's program: it can be a platform brand, a lender's own brand (when a single lender anchors or fully owns the waterfall), or white label — the merchant's own name on the program ("Lenovo Financing," "Wayfair Financing") with the platform running everything underneath. Support tip: the consumer only ever sees the brand — they usually don't know (and don't need to know) which lender or platform sits behind it, until they look at their credit agreement or statement.

Compliance guardrail

Under Reg Z advertising rules, stating a trigger term — a monthly payment amount, "0% APR for 12 months," a down-payment figure — obligates you to include the full set of required disclosures. That's why widget and estimator copy is lawyer-reviewed and why the fine print exists. Changing promo copy is never "just a copy change."

05

Checkout, account numbers & funding rails

What happens at acceptance, what an account number really is (legally and technically), and how a virtual card moves the money.

Two flows, two modes, three surfaces

Consumers reach financing through one of two flows. In the apply flow (apply-only), the consumer just applies — typically from the promotional widget/modal — and, if approved, uses the approved amount later in the merchant's checkout. In the checkout flow, the consumer applies while placing the order: they select the financing payment method at checkout, the modal opens, and they either apply-and-buy or locate an existing account (returning-consumer lookup + OTP, §03) and complete the purchase.

Independently of the flow, integrations run in one of two modes: platform-managed (the consumer enters personal information into the platform's own UI) or merchant-managed (the merchant's UI collects the data and the merchant's servers forward it to the platform's APIs). The consumer can't tell the difference — what changes is on the backend: who collects and owns the data, which drives privacy, PCI/PII handling, and contract terms.

Beyond the merchant's website, the same flows run on in-store and call-center surfaces: a sales app where a rep builds the cart and starts an application on the spot, and a send-link option (call-center API) that texts or emails the apply/checkout link to the customer's own device so they enter PII themselves.

What happens at acceptance

Consumer picks an offer → consents → hard pull (if not already done) → lender books the account → consumer e-signs the credit agreement → account number issued → checkout proceeds to authorization (§06).

E-signature & E-SIGN consent

legal checkpoint

Under the E-SIGN Act, electronic signatures and electronic disclosures are as legally valid as paper — but only after the consumer gives consent to electronic records (agreeing to receive legal documents electronically, with the ability to withdraw). That consent step is why signing flows start with a checkbox that feels redundant: it isn't UX, it's the legal foundation for everything signed after it.

What actually gets e-signed across our flows: the credit agreement (and its Summary of Terms) at booking; joint-intent confirmations on co-applicant flows; and, in home improvement, the certificate of completion that releases funds to the contractor (§10). Each signature carries an audit trail — timestamp, verified identity, evidence of intent — which is what makes it enforceable. Treat signature events as compliance artifacts, not form submissions: they must be stored, retrievable, and tied to the exact document version the consumer saw.

Account number — the legal & technical view

identifiers

Two very different species share the name:

Card PAN (Primary Account Number) — the 15–16-digit card number (PLCC, card-rail DLOC, VCC). Structure: BIN/IIN (first 6–8 digits identify the issuer/program) + account identifier + a Luhn check digit. PANs are PCI-DSS scoped: strict storage/transmission rules, tokenization instead of raw storage, masking for display (at most BIN + last 4), never storing CVV after authorization, key management, and audit scope. Architectural rule of thumb: if you can avoid touching raw PANs — delegating to the issuer-processor's tokens — you keep entire systems out of PCI scope.

Loan / line account number (non-card) — the lender's servicing identifier. Not PCI data, but it is nonpublic personal information (NPI) under GLBA: mask it in UIs and logs, encrypt at rest, restrict to need-to-know. It's what appears on statements, what the consumer pays against, and the key for servicing, disputes, and records-retention obligations.

For engineers

Keep a clean identifier map: platform application ID ↔ platform account & transaction IDs ↔ lender account ID ↔ processor token. Put idempotency keys on account creation and on every money-movement call — a retry must never double-book an account or double-charge a purchase. Support looks up accounts by last 4 plus identity verification, never by full number over the phone; full account numbers don't belong in emails, logs, analytics events, or screenshots.

Card rails 101 — network, issuer, acquirer

payments anatomy

Every card payment — a physical PLCC swipe or a VCC charge — crosses the same four roles. The card network (Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover) is the rails: it routes authorization and settlement messages between the parties and writes the rulebook for auths, refunds, and chargebacks. "Visa rails" just means transactions carried and governed by Visa's network. The issuer is the financial institution that issued the card and owns the cardholder account — it's the party that approves or declines every authorization. The issuer-processor (e.g., Marqeta) is the technology layer running card issuance and authorization processing on behalf of an issuing bank/program — this is who mints our virtual cards and fires the JIT funding calls. The acquirer (merchant acquirer/processor) sits on the merchant's side: it accepts card transactions for the merchant and receives its settlement money from the network.

Money mechanics on the rails: on each transaction the acquirer pays the issuer interchange (a network-set fee) — which is how issuing programs earn revenue and why funding purchases over card rails isn't free. The flow for one purchase: merchant → acquirer → network → issuer (approve?) → back down the same chain; settlement follows the same path with money, typically next day.

VCC & VCN — Virtual Credit Card funding

funding rail

The trick that lets a lender fund a purchase at any merchant without the merchant integrating with that lender: at checkout, the platform — via an issuer-processor such as Marqeta — issues a single-use virtual card funded by the lender, and the merchant simply charges that card on the card rails it already has.

VCC vs VCN: in practice the two are used interchangeably, but strictly the VCC (virtual credit card) is the card product — the account with its controls and funding — while the VCN (virtual card number) is the number itself: a real 16-digit PAN with expiry and CVV, existing only digitally, no plastic ever printed. The VCN is a first-class PAN — Luhn-valid, BIN-identified, routable on Visa/Mastercard rails, and fully PCI-scoped, so all the §"account number" handling rules apply to it.

JIT (just-in-time) funding: the card holds no standing balance; when the merchant authorizes it, the processor asks the funding source in real time to approve exactly that authorization. Controls enforce an exact or maximum amount, an expiry window, a merchant / MCC lock, and single use. Refunds flow back onto the VCC and up to the lender; captures can be partial — the postsale lifecycle (§06) applies symmetrically, and the VCC settlement report becomes one of the ledgers reconciliation must match (§07).

The alternative rail is direct integration / ACH settlement: the lender pays the merchant on a funding schedule — no card, but it requires per-lender merchant onboarding and payout logic.

06

Postsale — the transaction lifecycle

Everything after "approved" is a small state machine per purchase. Master it and you can debug most support tickets.

The object moving through this state machine is the charge — internal name for an order placed through the platform. Every successful checkout produces a charge, and every charge carries a payment status that walks the states below, in order: authorized → settled → (refunded / disputed), with void and expiry as the early exits.

Authorization (auth)

hold

A hold against the account's open-to-buy (or the loan amount). No money moves; available credit drops. Auths expire after a number of days (varies by product and rail) — a stale auth must be reauthorized before capture.

Settlement / capture

money moves

The merchant confirms fulfillment and the held amount — or part of it — becomes a real charge. Partial capture and multi-capture support split shipments: retailers settle at shipment, not at order placement. Only after settlement does the charge hit the consumer's billing cycle and start any promo clock.

Void / auth reversal

before settlement

Cancels an authorization before settlement: the hold is released and nothing ever appears on a statement. Cheap, clean, invisible to the consumer's bill. Watch out: not every lender supports voids — on those programs a transaction must settle and then be fully refunded, which does touch the statement. Know which lenders in the waterfall behave this way before advising a merchant or consumer.

Refund

after settlement

Returns money after settlement — a credit back to the account (and, on VCC rails, back up the chain to the lender). Full or partial. On promo plans, refunds reduce the promo balance according to the lender's allocation rules — a reliable source of consumer-confusion tickets ("I returned it, why is there still a balance?").

Chargeback / dispute

contested

The consumer disputes a settled charge with the issuer/lender ("item never arrived," "not my transaction"). On credit products, FCBA / Reg Z billing-error rights apply — generally the dispute must be raised within 60 days of the statement. Flow: dispute opened → provisional credit → merchant representment (evidence) → resolution, categorized by reason codes. Liability splits between merchant and lender by program rules and rail. A chargeback is money movement and a compliance workflow: it hits reconciliation, merchant debits, and reporting.

Reversal

umbrella term

The umbrella word people use loosely. An auth reversal is a void; a transaction/settlement reversal undoes a capture (often same-cycle); a funding reversal corrects money movement between institutions. When someone says "reversal," ask which object is being reversed.

07

Reconciliation

Four ledgers describe every purchase. Reconciliation is the recurring proof that they all agree.

On a multi-lender platform, at least four systems record the same transaction: the platform's ledger, the lender's funding and settlement reports, the merchant's order system, and — on VCC rails — the processor's settlement files. Reconciliation is the daily/monthly process of matching every auth, settle, refund, and chargeback, and every dollar funded, netted, or fee'd, across all of them.

How it works

  • Inputs: settlement files, funding/remittance reports, chargeback reports, fee invoices.
  • Matching: automated by transaction IDs, VCC token, amounts, and dates, with a breaks queue for exceptions.
  • Usual causes of breaks: timing (a refund crossing the funding cutoff), partial captures, amount mismatches (fees, rounding), missing records (retry/idempotency bugs), chargebacks landing in a different period.
  • Money mechanics: gross vs net settlement (are merchant fees withheld from funding, or invoiced separately?), lender discount/subsidy netting, chargeback debits.
For engineers

Reconciliation is the system's proof of correctness. A postsale bug — double capture, lost refund, dropped webhook — shows up here first. Treat recon breaks as production signals, not accounting trivia, and design money-movement APIs so every record carries the IDs the match engine needs.

Why the same application gets different treatment by state

Federal law sets the floor; states layer their own rules on top. The practical effect you'll see in the product: extra data collected in some states, different offers (or missing waterfall rungs) in others, and state-specific paperwork at signing. The recurring patterns:

  • Community property states (AZ, CA, ID, LA, NV, NM, TX, WA, WI): spouses share ownership of marital assets and debts, so lenders may collect marital status and spouse information that Reg B otherwise restricts — Wisconsin goes further with spousal-notification requirements. This is the classic "why does the form ask extra questions only in this state?"
  • Rate caps & licensing: each state licenses lenders and caps rates (usury). A high-APR second-look product may be repriced or simply absent in strict-cap states — so the same applicant sees a different waterfall depending on where they live.
  • Texas home equity: the state constitution puts hard limits on borrowing against a homestead (LTV cap, fee cap, a mandatory waiting period after application, limits on concurrent equity loans) — the canonical example of one state reshaping a whole product.
  • LTO carve-outs: a few states treat rent-to-own as a credit sale rather than a lease, so LTO lenders often don't operate there — the last waterfall rung disappears. California's cash-price early-buyout rule (§02) is another state-specific LTO twist.
  • State addenda & disclosures: credit agreements pick up state-specific inserts — different late-fee caps and grace periods, cooling-off rights, translation requirements (e.g., California requires contract translations when negotiation happened in certain non-English languages).

Treat the state lists above as illustrative for orientation, not as a compliance reference — the authoritative per-state matrix lives with each lender's program rules and our compliance team. The takeaway for builders: state is a first-class input to decisioning, offer presentation, and document generation, never just an address field.

End-to-end story

One purchase, every concept in this guide, in order.

Scenario A — the happy path

A homeowner needs a $6,400 HVAC replacement, quoted in-home by a franchise technician. The technician shows monthly options from the payment estimator ("from $118/mo"). The homeowner scans a QR code → prequalification (soft pull) → the platform sweeps the waterfall: the prime bank declines (thin file), a near-prime installment lender approves $7,000, and the second-look lender approves $6,500 at a higher APR.

The homeowner picks the near-prime offer on an 18-month 0% equal-pay rate plan (merchant-subsidized) → consents → hard pull → the lender books the account, the agreement is e-signed. The platform issues a VCC locked to the merchant for $6,400; the technician charges it — authorization now, capture after installation. Two weeks later a $400 part is returned → partial refund to the account. At month-end, reconciliation matches platform ↔ lender funding report ↔ VCC settlement file; one break (the refund crossed the funding cutoff) auto-clears next cycle.

Scenario B — the recovery paths

Same flow, but the bureau returns a frozen file → the UX names the bureau to thaw, and the application resumes. Still declined alone on DTI → the spouse joins as a co-applicant (joint intent captured per Reg B) → approved. Or: the order is canceled before shipment → void, nothing ever reaches a statement. Or: the consumer doesn't recognize a charge → dispute / chargeback, provisional credit issued while the merchant represents evidence.

The home-improvement vertical

Financing sold at the kitchen table, funded in stages, and released by a signature — the vocabulary of our biggest vertical.

Home improvement (HI) is different from retail in three ways that reshape the whole financing flow: the purchase is goods plus labor (part of the ticket isn't a leaseable, returnable thing), the work spans weeks or months (money can't all move on day one), and the sale happens in the home (a technician with a tablet, not a checkout page). Everything in this section exists because of those three facts.

The cast

roles

The homeowner is the applicant/borrower. The contractor — also called a dealer, installer, or (in franchise home-services networks) a technician — is the merchant: they sell the job, offer the financing, and get funded. Larger contractors add a sales rep or finance manager who runs the financing conversation. The contractor works out of a contractor portal (dealer portal / merchant portal): one place to start applications (or send an apply link to the customer's phone), see plans and promos, request funding and stage payments, and process refunds and change orders. Different lender programs use different words for the same role — knowing that dealer = contractor = technician's company saves a lot of confusion in tickets.

Project lifecycle & staged funding

money over time

The flow: inspection → quote/estimate → financing offered on the spot → approval with a purchase window (§04 — the loan stays open for new charges while the project runs) → work begins with staged funding → change orders adjust the amount when scope shifts → certificate of completion → final disbursement.

Staged funding (milestone disbursement) releases money in slices tied to project progress: an early draw gives the contractor procurement funds for materials, later stage payments follow the work, and the final slice waits for the homeowner's sign-off. It protects both sides — the contractor isn't fronting material costs, and the homeowner isn't paying in full for unfinished work. For the platform, every stage payment is a partial capture (§06) with its own reconciliation footprint (§07).

Certificate of completion

disbursement control

A legally binding sign-off — usually e-signed (§05) — in which the homeowner confirms the work is done as agreed. It is the trigger that authorizes the platform/lender to release final funds to the contractor: a safety valve for the lender, proof-of-work for the contractor.

Why fintech programs insist on it: it prevents paying contractors for incomplete or abandoned jobs, it captures explicit customer authorization before money moves, and it protects the funding bank partners from disbursing against faulty or unfinished work. Support pattern to know: "the job is done but the contractor hasn't been paid" almost always means a completion certificate is unsigned or stuck.

In-home selling protection

Because HI financing is often sold door-to-door or in the home, the FTC's cooling-off rule for home-solicitation sales applies: the consumer generally has a 3-day right of rescission to cancel. Flows must surface the cancellation right and hold irreversible money movement until the window passes — one more reason funding is staged rather than instant.

Adjacent vertical worth one line: solar financing (the solar flag on plans) — same contractor-sold, staged-funding model, but with its own plan shapes built around the federal solar tax credit and system-activation milestones.

Agentic commerce

Where checkout is heading: AI agents that discover, compare, and buy on the consumer's behalf — and what that means for embedded financing.

Agentic commerce is the emerging model where an AI agent (a ChatGPT-style assistant, a shopping copilot) doesn't just recommend a product — it completes the purchase for the consumer. Discovery, comparison, and checkout move from the merchant's website into the agent conversation. The industry projection making the rounds: a third of enterprises adopting agent-based AI by 2028, from under 1% a couple of years ago.

ACP — Agentic Commerce Protocol

emerging standard

An open-source standard (Apache 2.0, initiated by Stripe and OpenAI) for letting agents transact with businesses. The moving parts: merchants expose agent-ready product feeds and a checkout API (REST and MCP); the buyer's payment credential is passed to the agent as a delegated token so the agent never sees the underlying card; and the merchant stays merchant of record — it keeps control of pricing, products, fulfillment, and the customer relationship. Fraud systems learn to distinguish legitimate agent-initiated transactions from bot abuse. Note the rhyme with our world: delegated payment tokens are the same pattern as tokenized PANs and JIT-funded VCCs (§05) — a credential scoped to one use, one amount, one counterparty.

What an agent needs from us

agent anatomy

The standard anatomy of a commerce agent: a role (what it's for), data (structured, machine-readable product and pricing information), actions (APIs it can call), guardrails (what it must not do), and channels (where it lives). The recurring lesson from every vendor: agents are only as good as the structured data behind them — an agent can't quote financing from a marketing banner.

Implications for embedded financing

why we care

If checkout happens inside an agent, financing must be a machine-readable payment method: plans, estimators, and prequalification exposed as APIs an agent can call — not just widgets a human sees. Three consequences to keep in mind:

  • Offers as data: "as low as $58/mo" must be computable and quotable by an agent, with the Reg Z trigger-term disclosures (§04) delivered inside the agent conversation — advertising compliance follows the offer wherever it's rendered.
  • The human checkpoints stay human: a credit application is made by the consumer, not their agent. Consent to a credit pull, KYC, and the e-signature on the credit agreement (§05) remain moments where the flow must reach the actual person — OTP and e-sign become the bridges between an agent-driven journey and a legally valid loan.
  • The rails are ready: agent payments built on delegated, single-use, amount-locked tokens are structurally what VCC/JIT funding already does — a multi-lender platform that can mint a scoped credential for one purchase is well positioned to plug financing into agent checkout.
